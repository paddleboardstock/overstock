## How EU Market Surveillance Works

Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 strengthened market surveillance across the European Union. National authorities in each member state can inspect products at any point — at customs, at warehouses, at dealers, or even post-sale. For recreational craft, surveillance focuses on whether CE-marked boats actually meet the requirements of 2013/53/EU.

The most common trigger for inspection is a competitor complaint, a consumer incident report, or a random customs check on imported vessels.

## What Inspectors Look For

During a surveillance inspection, authorities typically request:

- **The technical file** — complete documentation showing how the boat meets each essential requirement
- **Declaration of Conformity** — properly signed and referencing correct directives and standards
- **CE marking and builder plate** — verified against the technical file data
- **Owner manual** — compliant with ISO 10240 and in the correct language
- **Production control evidence** — quality system records for the applicable conformity module

Inspectors compare these documents against each other and against the physical product. Inconsistencies are red flags.

## Consequences of Incomplete Technical Files

The consequences escalate based on the severity of the non-compliance:

### Product Withdrawal

Authorities can order immediate withdrawal of non-compliant products from the market. This means the manufacturer or importer must recall all affected units from distribution channels and end users.

### Fines and Financial Penalties

Member states set their own penalty levels, but fines for RCD non-compliance can reach tens of thousands of euros per violation. Repeat offenders face escalating penalties.

### Import Bans

For products from third countries, authorities can impose import bans at the EU border. Once a product is flagged, all future shipments are subject to detention until compliance is proven.

### Public Notification

Under the EU RAPEX system, non-compliant products are listed in a public database visible to consumers, competitors, and authorities across all member states. This creates lasting reputational damage.

### Criminal Liability

In cases involving falsified documentation or deliberate misrepresentation, criminal proceedings may follow. Directors and responsible persons can face personal liability.

## Common Documentation Failures

The most frequent issues found during inspections include:

- **Missing test reports** — stability or buoyancy calculations not included in the file
- **Inconsistent builder plate data** — maximum load or engine power on the plate does not match the technical file
- **Outdated standards references** — technical files citing superseded standard versions
- **No Declaration of Conformity** — or a DoC that lists incorrect directive numbers
- **Incomplete owner manuals** — missing emergency procedures or language requirements not met
- **No production control records** — particularly for modules requiring quality assurance evidence

## Ensuring Audit Readiness

To survive a surveillance inspection, manufacturers and importers should:

- Conduct internal documentation audits at least annually
- Verify that builder plate data matches technical file values exactly
- Maintain a quick-access summary of the technical file for rapid response to authority requests
- Train staff on which documents to provide and how to interact with inspectors
