## GRP Hulls in the RIB Market

Glass Reinforced Plastic (GRP) hulls dominate the RIB market, offering an excellent strength-to-weight ratio, design flexibility, and corrosion resistance. But GRP is a complex composite material whose properties depend entirely on manufacturing quality. Without proper testing and certification, a GRP hull may look identical to a compliant one but fail catastrophically in service.

## Required Test Reports

### Gelcoat Thickness Testing

The gelcoat is the first line of defense against water ingress and UV degradation. Test reports must verify:

- **Minimum thickness** — typically 0.4-0.6 mm for marine applications
- **Uniformity** — variation across the hull surface must fall within specified tolerances
- **Adhesion to laminate** — the bond between gelcoat and structural layers must resist peel forces

Gelcoat that is too thin allows osmosis; gelcoat that is too thick cracks under impact.

### Layup Verification

The structural laminate must match the design specification. Verification includes:

- **Fiber type and weight** — woven roving, biaxial, or unidirectional at specified grams per square meter
- **Resin type and content** — orthophthalic, isophthalic, or vinyl ester with fiber-to-resin ratio typically 30:70 to 40:60 by weight
- **Laminate schedule** — number and orientation of layers at each hull location
- **Core material** — if sandwich construction is used, core density and shear strength must be verified

### Impact Resistance Testing

RIBs operate at speed in rough water where hull slamming is routine. Impact testing measures:

- **Drop weight impact** — a standardized weight dropped onto hull samples to simulate slamming loads
- **Residual strength post-impact** — the hull must retain structural integrity after minor impact damage
- **Puncture resistance** — particularly important for the keel and chine areas

### Osmosis Testing

Osmosis — blistering caused by water penetration into the laminate — is a chronic GRP problem. Testing includes:

- **Barcol hardness** — surface hardness as an indicator of cure quality
- **Boiling water test** — accelerated aging that reveals susceptibility to blistering
- **Moisture content measurement** — using capacitance meters to detect trapped moisture in the laminate

Hulls built with isophthalic or vinyl ester resins show significantly better osmosis resistance than those using general-purpose orthophthalic resin.

### Structural Strength Testing

Full-scale or sub-scale structural tests verify that the hull withstands design loads:

- **Bending stiffness** — the hull resists deflection under distributed load
- **Torsional rigidity** — resistance to twisting forces from wave action
- **Local reinforcement verification** — around engine transoms, lifting points, and deck hardware

## GRP vs Aluminum Hull Testing Differences

Aluminum hulls require a different testing focus:

- **Weld quality testing** — radiographic or ultrasonic inspection of welds replaces laminate verification
- **Corrosion testing** — salt spray and galvanic corrosion tests instead of osmosis
- **Fatigue testing** — aluminum is more susceptible to fatigue cracking at stress concentrations
- **Hardness testing** — Brinell or Vickers instead of Barcol

GRP testing emphasizes material consistency and cure quality; aluminum testing emphasizes joint integrity and corrosion resistance.

## Certification and the Technical File

All test reports must be included in the RCD technical file. Reports should reference:

- The specific hull model and build configuration
- Test standards applied (ISO, EN ISO, or equivalent)
- Pass/fail criteria and actual results
- Test laboratory accreditation details
