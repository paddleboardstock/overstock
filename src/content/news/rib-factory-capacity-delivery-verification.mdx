## Why Capacity Verification Matters

When a buyer sources RIB boats from a factory, the stated production capacity is only as credible as the evidence supporting it. Overpromised capacity leads to missed delivery dates, quality shortcuts, and damaged relationships. Professional buyers use factory audits to separate genuine capability from sales exaggeration.

## Production Planning Systems

A factory that cannot show how it plans production cannot reliably deliver on schedule. Auditors look for:

- **ERP or MRP systems** — software-based planning tools that track orders, material requirements, and production schedules
- **Production boards** — visual management tools showing current work orders, progress status, and upcoming changeovers
- **Capacity planning reports** — demonstrating that the factory has calculated throughput per line per shift and can accommodate the buyer's order volume

Factories relying solely on informal scheduling and verbal commitments are a delivery risk.

## Workforce Scheduling and Skills

Capacity is not just about floor space — it is about people with the right skills available at the right time. Auditors verify:

- **Total workforce count** broken down by function (lamination, tube fabrication, assembly, quality control)
- **Skill certification records** — especially for critical processes like Hypalon adhesive bonding and GRP lamination
- **Shift patterns** — single shift vs multi-shift operation and the ability to scale up
- **Turnover rates** — high turnover disrupts skill continuity and training investment

A factory with 50 workers but 30% annual turnover has less effective capacity than one with 40 stable, experienced employees.

## Material Stock Levels

Production cannot proceed without materials. Auditors examine:

- **Current inventory** of key inputs — PVC or Hypalon fabric, resin, gelcoat, adhesive, valves, hardware
- **Supplier lead times** — how long it takes to replenish each material
- **Safety stock policies** — minimum stock levels that trigger reorders
- **Storage conditions** — temperature and humidity control for adhesives and resins that degrade in poor conditions

A factory that orders materials only after receiving a purchase order adds supplier lead time to the production lead time.

## Current Order Pipeline

An empty factory may look capable of instant production but could indicate business problems. Conversely, a fully loaded factory may not have capacity for new orders. Auditors review:

- **Active order list** — current commitments, quantities, and due dates
- **Capacity utilization rate** — percentage of available production time already allocated
- **Seasonal patterns** — whether the factory experiences peak periods that compress available capacity

## Presenting Realistic Lead Times

The most credible factories present lead times that account for:

- Material procurement time
- Production queue time
- Quality inspection and testing time
- Packaging and shipping preparation
- A realistic contingency buffer

Lead times stated as "4-6 weeks" with supporting schedule evidence are far more credible than "3 weeks" with no documentation.

## Documentation to Prepare

Before a buyer audit, factories should have ready:

- Production floor layout with capacity per area
- 12-month production schedule showing current commitments
- Workforce roster with skill classifications
- Material inventory report with stock levels and reorder points
- On-time delivery performance data for the past year
